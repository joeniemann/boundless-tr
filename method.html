Week by week

The default arc,
adjusted to you.

Visit 1 · 90 min

Evaluation

History first, then testing. We find out what the tissue can tolerate today and what the rest of the chain is doing to compensate. You leave the room with your numbers and the plan built on them.

  • Full-body movement screen and joint-by-joint range
  • Strength and symmetry testing on the involved side
  • Your goal activity defined in specifics, not vibes
Weeks 1–4

Rebuild the base

Calm what's irritated, restore the range you're missing, and rebuild the capacity the injured area quietly lost. Hands-on work shows up only where it earns its place — everything else is loaded movement.

  • Range restored to within 5° of the healthy side
  • Pain predictable and controlled during loading
  • Homework: two to three short sessions a week
Weeks 4–10

Load it

The phase most plans never reach. Progressive loading against measured targets, then speed and direction change. This is where rehab stops being rehab and becomes training — and where the gap actually closes.

  • Heavy strength work through full range
  • Plyometric and reactive progressions
  • Re-tested every two weeks against your baseline
Weeks 10–12

Test and release

Sport-specific testing under fatigue, because that's when things go wrong. If you pass, you get a written program to keep. If you don't, we know exactly which number is short and how long it takes to fix.

  • Return-to-sport battery, fatigued and fresh
  • Written program you own after discharge
  • Check-in scheduled at six weeks out

What gets measured

No number,
no opinion.

Limb symmetry

Side-to-side strength and power, tested in the positions your sport actually demands rather than a single seated angle.

Target · ≥ 90% of healthy side

Range of motion

Measured joint by joint, plus the end-range control that tells us whether the range is usable under load.

Target · within 5° both sides

Force output

Peak force and rate of force development. Slow strength returns first; fast strength is what's usually still missing at discharge.

Tracked every two weeks

Hop & land

Single-leg hop distance, landing quality, and how well you absorb force on repeat contacts when you're tired.

Target · symmetry + clean mechanics

Endurance capacity

Reps to fatigue in the positions that matter. Most re-injuries happen late in a game, not in the first five minutes.

Tested fresh and fatigued

Confidence

A validated self-report score. If your body passes but your head doesn't trust it, you're not ready and the data should say so.

Scored at each re-test

A session, start to finish · 60 minutes, 1:1

Where the hour goes.

0–10 min

Check in and re-test

What happened since last time, how the homework felt, and one or two quick measures so we're both working from today's numbers instead of last month's.

10–20 min

Prepare the tissue

Targeted hands-on work and mobility — but only where it buys a better position for the loading that follows. It is never the whole session.

20–50 min

Load and train

The main event. Strength, power, or sport-specific work at the intensity your current phase calls for, coached rep by rep.

50–60 min

Debrief and program

What changed, what's next, and exactly what to do between now and the next visit — written down, not explained once and forgotten.

One case, in full · anonymized

Shoulder, overhead athlete.

Labral repair · 24-year-old pitcher

Cleared at ten weeks.
Nowhere near ready.

Discharged from a previous clinic pain-free with full range — and no plan for throwing. He could lift his arm overhead. He could not decelerate it. Those are entirely different problems, and only one of them ends a career.

Intake

What we found

External rotation strength at 61% of the other side and rate of force development well below where it needed to be. Range looked fine, which is exactly why he'd been discharged.

ER strength61%
Decel control44%

Rebuild

Heavy isometrics into full-range strength work for the cuff and scapular chain, plus the trunk and hip work every throwing shoulder needs and rarely gets.

ER strength79%

Weeks 5–11

Load and throw

Plyometric ball work, then a staged throwing progression tied to test results rather than the calendar. Two weeks were repeated when the numbers stalled.

Released

Passed the full battery fatigued, cleared to a competitive mound with a written in-season maintenance program and a check-in six weeks out.

Discharge standards

What "ready"
has to mean.

Symmetry, not sensation

Strength and power within 10% of the uninvolved side — measured, not estimated by how it feels on a good day.

Holds under fatigue

You repeat the battery tired. Mechanics that fall apart at rep twenty are mechanics that fall apart in the fourth quarter.

Sport-specific, not generic

The final tests look like your actual sport — the cut, the throw, the lift — not a hallway walk and a thumbs up.

You believe it

Confidence is scored. If you don't trust the limb yet, we keep working, because hesitation is its own injury risk.

You leave with a program

A written plan for staying there. Discharge is the start of maintenance, not the end of the work.

The door stays open

A scheduled check-in six weeks out, and a standing answer to questions after that.
